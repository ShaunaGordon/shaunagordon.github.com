I have a love-hate relationship with this current generation of {{% abbrev AI "Artificial Intelligence" %}} tools.

Don't get me wrong, I love the *idea* and the *potential* of the technology. I mean, what nerd doesn't want their own J.A.R.V.I.S.?

![Tony Stark smirking with JARVIS HUD flickering around](/images/tony-stark-jarvis.gif)

But we're not there yet. Not even close. And what we do have is largely getting shoved down our throats by companies trying to justify the money they're hemorrhaging to keep these behemoth models afloat. Frankly, most of the issues I have with this generation of AI tools...aren't actually about the tech, but about the people at the center of the tech and the subsequent hype. That's not to say, however, that the tech itself doesn't have limitations that people ought to remember when using it.

## Pay No Attention To The Man Behind The Curtain...

Remember the classic _Wizard of Oz_ movie? Remember when our intrepid travelers reach the wizard, and he's this giant floating head with great mystical powers? ...except he isn't a giant floating head. He's just a guy, with no magic to speak of, who made a clever hologram.

That's the current generation of generative {{% abbrev AI "Artificial Intelligence" %}}.

I'm not even joking.

At the core of *everything* in this generation of {{% abbrev AI "Artificial Intelligence" %}}, behind the mountain of glorified chatbots named Copilot, behind the shiny text editors and all the "AI-powered!" everything, are large language models (and, currently to a lesser degree, small language models, but we'll get to those), or in the case of the image generators, latent diffusion models (basically, the image version of {{% abbrev LLMs "Large Language Models" %}}). You know, ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, etc. And what these are, are very sophisticated, but *horribly* inefficient probability machines.

Natural language processing and computational linguistics expert [Emily Bender, and her colleagues, called them "stochastic parrots"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stochastic_parrot) in their paper on {{% abbrev LLMs "Large Language Models" %}} a few years ago, and [still maintain](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=N5c2X8vhfBE) that the new versions since the paper's publication remain so. Considering the fundamental technology hasn't changed between, say, GPT 3 and GPT 4.5 or whatever the version is now, it makes sense.

No matter how much you polish a turd ([and you can, in fact, polish a turd](https://www.dailymotion.com/video/x2lrkhj)), it's still a turd.

But why is it so convincing, then, if it doesn't really *understand* language? In short, because humans are *also* very sophisticated probability machines. The human brain actively seeks out patterns, and has a tendency to try to anthropomorphize things. It turns out that human language at the word prediction level is...[also kind of predictable](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7064512/). 

[As Emily Bender puts it](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=N5c2X8vhfBE):

> When we encounter text in a language in which we are proficient, we can't help but make sense of it. So, when we encounter synthetic text stitched together by one of those stochastic parrots, we still make sense of it. In other words, coherence is in the eye of the beholder.

These things *seem* trustworthy, because they seem to make sense to us, and -- as [OpenAI apparently just learned](https://openai.com/index/expanding-on-sycophancy/), while [Anthropic seemed to know for a while](https://www.anthropic.com/news/towards-understanding-sycophancy-in-language-models) -- language models are great big sycophants. Humans, it turns out, really, *really* want things to make sense, [and will choose a confident, comfortable lie over an uncomfortable truth](https://medium.com/@ShotsOfRhapsody/the-psychology-of-belief-why-humans-prefer-comfort-over-truth-a9787ad7f605). We also trust things more, when they look and sound human (unless they hit the [Uncanny Valley](https://spectrum.ieee.org/what-is-the-uncanny-valley)), and despite a certain "soullessness" that many of us can pick up from the generated output of these models, they seem remarkably human-like (at least if you don't look too hard at them).

## Hallucinations: Mansplaining As A Service

I once saw generative {{% abbrev AI "Artificial Intelligence" %}} described as "mansplaining as a service," because of its tendency to be so confidently, insistently, and objectively wrong. Even worse, it can [concede passive-aggressively](https://futurism.com/artificial-intelligence-automated-mansplaining-machine), if it concedes at all. This has to be one of my favorite descriptors of it, to be honest. Thankfully, not *all* LLM tools are usually like this, since some of the organizations are responsible enough to put in safeguards.

### When It's Just Plain Wrong

One of the many times -- and probably the most persistent example -- I've run into this was when I got to play with Discord's piloted AI bot, Clyde. I decided to see what all I could do with it, since Discord was...a little opaque about it. I was hoping for a more holistic bot that could replace the other general-purpose, but far less sophisticated bots like Mee6 and Carl, allowing me to use natural language to do tasks that previously relied on rigid keywords.

Was it the most advanced usage of such a tool? Maybe not, but it would have been quite useful, and that's the whole point, right?

Even so, what I got...wasn't that.

My first mistake, apparently, was asking Clyde what Clyde could do.

![Clyde, can you do reminders? Why yes, Shauna, I can](/images/clyde-reminders-01.png)

Oh, look! It says it can! Great, let's set a reminder.

![Clyde, set me a reminder for tonight and tomorrow at 6:30pm](/images/clyde-reminders-02.png)

Clyde even confirmed it, which was great.

...except when the reminder time came around... I didn't get a reminder. So, I probed Clyde to see why.

I started by asking if it knew the time. Kind of crucial information to have if one is going to serve as a reminder bot.

![I'm sorry, I don't have access to the time outside of the chat timestamp](/images/clyde-reminders-03.png)

Well, that's... okay then...

Now I was just curious about how it would respond if I tried digging deeper, and if I could get it to admit it ~lied to me~ hallucinated (spoiler: it never did).

So, how can Clyde do reminders, if it can't tell the time? Magic, apparently.

![How can you do reminders if you don't have access to a clock? I can still set reminders based on the time you set in the message](/images/clyde-reminders-04.png)

I even tried calling it out explicitly, and in true mansplaining as a service fashion, Clyde...blamed it on the Discord API. Now, mind you, Discord bots can, in fact, do reminders. There are several out there with this capability, and they're perfectly reliable (bot or Discord downtime notwithstanding).

![My reminder didn't go off. I'm sorry, Shauna, I'm limited by the Discord API and can't guarantee that reminders will always work](/images/clyde-reminders-05.png)

What's *even better* about this exchange was that not only did it *not* admit in thread that it can't actually do reminders, but I accidentally to got it *to* admit to a different thing it had previously said it could do *of its own accord* (submit feedback), *and then* (!!) accidentally got it to admit that it doesn't, in fact, have reminder capability.

![Clyde, do you have settings I can adjust? I'm sorry, I don't, but if you have any suggestions or feedback, don't hesitate to let me know!](/images/clyde-feedback-01.png)

Surprise, surprise, that was a hallucination, too.

![Clyde, you really did say that you had a reminder feature. ...No I didn't.](/images/clyde-feedback-02.png)

Moral of the story? Never trust the {{% abbrev LLM "Large Language Model" %}}.

What makes this so insidious is the fact that it can be accurate somewhere in the ballpark of about [70-80% of the time,](https://research.aimultiple.com/ai-hallucination/) depending on the subject, method of information retrieval, and questions asked, and because of that, it takes having at least foundational knowledge of the topic at hand to know when it's in that other 20-30%, because the LLM doesn't know it's incorrect and therefore asserts it with the same perceived confidence as correct responses. Researchers and developers can possibly tweak and fine-tune it to incrementally improve those numbers, but as long as the fundamental technology is based on word probability, and the machine has no sense of correctness, those errors will always be present.

Now, consider how much misinformation and disinformation exists on the Internet. Combine that with intentional [data poisoning](https://www.crowdstrike.com/en-us/cybersecurity-101/cyberattacks/data-poisoning/) (which isn't just used in the traditional cyberattack context, but also as [defense against unlicensed use of human content in training data](https://nightshade.cs.uchicago.edu/whatis.html)), and it's easy to see how quickly quality can degrade.

### But Hallucinations Can Be Fixed...Right?

Unfortunately, hallucinations are the result of the underlying {{% abbrev LLM "Large Language Model" %}} technology. As sophisticated probability machines, they determine the *most likely* next word (well, token, technically) in the sequence. The catch here is that "most likely" is not guaranteed to be correct, and can never be guaranteed to be correct.

Now, it *can* be mitigated, using other technologies -- {{% abbrev RAG "Retrieval Augmented Generation" %}} systems can use [rerankers](https://www.chatbase.co/blog/reranking) to improve the overall quality of results, for example -- but even that's rather limited. Search for how to mitigate hallucination rates, and you'll find nearly all methods fall squarely on the shoulders of the end user -- use {{% abbrev RAG "Retrieval Augmented Generation" %}}, use human fact-checkers, use human feedback reinforcement, etc. That's because it *can't* really be solved at the core functionality level, because it's a limitation of the technology, itself. Trying to keep a language model from hallucinating is like trying to keep a computer from giving a not-quite-accurate decimal conversion for \({1 \over 3}\) -- to do so, you have to destroy the core functionality of it. In both cases, they can only be papered over after the fact.

#### Hallucination Rates Are Actually Getting Worse, And Will Continue To Worsen

[Hallucination rates for bigger, newer models have worsened,](https://www.newscientist.com/article/2479545-ai-hallucinations-are-getting-worse-and-theyre-here-to-stay/) and the data suggests that we've reached the point where [throwing more power and data at them](https://www.newscientist.com/article/2449427-ais-get-worse-at-answering-simple-questions-as-they-get-bigger/) can no longer improve them (and can actually be counterproductive).

There's also the issue of diminishing returns for these massive models. It takes increasingly more data to get increasingly smaller improvements, and as the content of their main source of data (the Internet) becomes increasingly saturated with {{% abbrev AI "Artificial Intelligence" %}} generated data, the worse quality the data is.

## Large Scale Generative {{% abbrev AI "Artificial Intelligence" %}} Is A Bubble

There, I said it. I might make a dedicated post about this, because this is probably the single most important issue about this whole thing.

Remember the 2008 recession? It was caused by subprime mortgage lending practices, giving larger loans to people than what they could afford. When it inevitably ended up in a bunch of houses getting foreclosed upon and loans to default, everything went belly-up.

We see basically the same thing with these large generative {{% abbrev AI "Artificial Intelligence" %}} companies like OpenAI and Anthropic. We're also starting to see early signs that [the bubble is about to burst](https://www.wheresyoured.at/power-cut/), despite Altman's claims that OpenAI's revenue will grow by orders of magnitude this year.

### {{% abbrev GenAI "Generative Artificial Intelligence" %}} Companies Burn (Everyone Else's) Cash

Have you ever wondered why Microsoft has suddenly started shoving its "Copilot" stuff into literally everything? GitHub, VS Code, Windows, Edge, Office, and has increasingly forced it upon users (as I write this, I have to contend with constantly excising Copilot from my VS Code, which, not unlike Internet Explorer, I can't wholly remove, because reasons). It's everywhere, and it's not even done well in most places (GitHub Copilot actually isn't too bad). It's all disjointed (the Copilot on Windows isn't the same Copilot in VS Code or on GitHub, technically), and its usefulness is questionable in most contexts.

But they *have* to do that, to justify the money they're hemorrhaging into OpenAI. Microsoft, for all intents and purposes, owns OpenAI at this point (strictly speaking, they only own 49% of the company, but OpenAI would have gone under, were it not for Microsoft keeping them afloat). The problem, though, is that OpenAI loses more money per year than it makes. Even after [$4 billion in revenue, it lost $5 billion in 2024,](https://www.wheresyoured.at/wheres-the-money/) which means it has to somehow more than double its revenue without increasing costs *just to break even*, because its return on investment is around **negative 225%**.

But here's the other problem -- unlike most things, where economy of scale starts kicking in at some point, and things become cheaper per unit, ChatGPT costs *more* the more its used. Some $5 billion of their $9 billion in operating costs comes from compute for either training or running the models. Even if the training cost is fixed, the running cost *isn't*, because each query to the model costs a certain amount, so the more queries, the higher the cost.

The only way for them to even get close to being viable with their current scheme is if they start heavily relying on the "gym membership" dark pattern, where a bunch of people have subscriptions, but don't actually use the service, and even then, it'd have to be something like a 3-to-1 ratio of non-using subscribers to actual, active users, because the alternative is tripling their subscription prices, unless they can dramatically reduce resource usage (something they claim to have done, but have no real evidence of).

In nearly any other scenario, nobody in their right mind would even *consider* pouring this much money into a company that burns it so hotly. But Sam Altman is really good at blowing smoke up people's asses with promises of artificial general intelligence just around the corner -- a promise that can never be delivered with LLMs, but executives are eating up, because of their fantasies of replacing human workers with computers, en masse.

### OpenAI's Empty Promises Are What's Costing Jobs

The majority of people who actually use LLM tools *and* understand them, will tell you that they're basically glorified autocomplete. They have their uses, certainly, but generally, they're only about as good as an intern and take about as much babysitting. Yet executives across the board have jumped head-first into the hype Kool-Aid, and are convinced that these tools are the future in the same way they believed mass outsourcing was the future (the really dumb part is so many of these execs are the same damned people; clearly they didn't learn).

So, like the outsourcing-happy execs of yore, today's execs have kicked half or more of their engineers, writers, QA people, marketers, and more to the curb and have used that money (and more, likely) to funnel into the umpteen Copilot applications in an effort to make more profits for their shareholders, while not only putting out lower-quality artifacts, but also taking chunks out of consumer confidence and buying power (thus hurting their own bottom line in the process).

### We're Already Drowning in Slop

What would happen if all of these companies achieved their dream of replacing all of their creators with generative AI?

[Model collapse.](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-024-07566-y)

Because LLMs can't discern correct from incorrect, *and* their output quality is inevitably lower than their source material (due to compression in order to handle the unfathomable amount of data needed to train them), its degradation as the overall content pool becomes increasingly saturated with AI generations is basically inevitable.

I've seen this one first hand, too, and it doesn't take a next-gen AI to trigger it, just the output of the tool, fed back into itself. It's shockingly easy to trigger in image generators like Midjourney, if you need inspiration for some eldritch abominations.

The ramvan has got to be my all-time favorite of these abominations I ended up with when I tried to get a steampunk/clockwork ram for an artificer D&D character I made some time back, after having no luck with human-made art.

![The ramvan is my all-time favorite](/images/eldritch-horror-01.png)

At one point, I had something close to what I wanted, but wanted to try to make some tweaks. So I fed the image I liked back into the generator to try to get it to use and modify it. It...gave me these...

![This is also what happens when you feed output into it](/images/eldritch-horror-02.png)

This particular image came about from operator error (Midjourney was relatively new at the time, and manipulating existing images was either arcane or not at all supported yet), but it's something that can easily be triggered on any image generation model that takes input images. I've done this on other platforms, too, where I've fed the generated image back into the model. The result is always...weird.

And...this is supposed to be ✨The Future✨...

## They Are Ethically Sketchy

It's not the fault of the tech, itself, but how the big players have obtained the massive amounts of data needed to train these models, and what the culture has encouraged them to be used for. [Legally, it's a minefield](https://iapp.org/news/a/generative-ai-and-intellectual-property-copyright-implications-for-ai-inputs-outputs) that courts are just starting to try to navigate, and will probably continue to navigate for years to come.

[It's an ethical minefield](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11023-024-09694-w), too. The big LLMs are notoriously trained on content that they didn't have rights to, and have shown to have had various kinds of private data leaked into it. Many companies are starting [to collect and use customer data to train their AI models,](https://www.darkreading.com/cyber-risk/vendors-training-ai-customer-data-enterprise-risk) even changing their terms of service under the customers. Then, there's the matter of other creative areas getting flooded with AI-generated...frankly...slop, which not only inevitably contains copyrighted source material, pulled directly from said source, but also crowds out the human-made stuff. Have you *seen* Pinterest lately?

Other fields and sites aren't immune, either. [Amazon has been flooded with AI generated garbage books](https://www.npr.org/2024/03/13/1237888126/growing-number-ai-scam-books-amazon). They've cracked down on such "books" since it started a couple of years ago, with things like mandatory disclosure of AI generation and limits on the number of books per author, per day, but their success is...limited. The mandatory disclosure is only visible to Amazon, and there's no way for the user to filter them out. Worse, even though it's "mandatory," it's still largely on the honor system. Sure, Amazon might find some and remove them for not disclosing being AI, but with *so much* out there, it's virtually impossible to make any significant inroads on enforcement.

## Agents Are A Privacy Nightmare

The latest mini-fad in this giant hype train is "Agentic {{% abbrev AI "Artificial Intelligence" %}}," semi-autonomous bots that can interact with myriad systems to do almost any imaginable task.

There's just one problem, [they can be a privacy nightmare](https://disesdi.substack.com/p/whos-afraid-of-agentic-ai). The whole point of agentic {{% abbrev AI "Artificial Intelligence" %}} is to shunt work off to the machine and let it do its thing. This is *awesome*, in theory. In practice, though, because what we currently call {{% abbrev AI "Artificial Intelligence" %}} has no concept of...anything, really -- right/wrong, public/private information, etc -- it has no way of saying "wait, this looks sketchy, I shouldn't expose this, and should let the user know."

This gets even worse [the more you shunt off to the machine](https://www.corporatecomplianceinsights.com/no-strings-attached-agentic-ai-tests-privacy-antitrust-boundaries/) and can even get into *antitrust* issues. Yikes.
